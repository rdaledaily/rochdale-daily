The priest, the chicken and the shoe: Rochdale's strangest boggart story

How an old Clegg Hall tale — in which a priest answers a spirit demanding ‘a body and a soul’ with a cock’s body and a shoe’s sole — survives as folklore even where documentary evidence does not.

By Rochdale Daily 19 Oct 2026 · 11:20 UTC

Clegg Hall stands on Clegg Hall Road, near Milnrow and Littleborough; the present house is a 17th‑century stone building listed Grade II* and recorded by Historic England as being built c.1610 (the site itself is older). ([historicengland.org.uk](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1309615))

Around that house, local tradition preserves one of the region’s more peculiar boggart stories: a wicked relation murders the lawful heirs, the hall becomes haunted by the children’s ghosts, and a pious priest bargains with the spirit — which insists on “a body and a soul” — only for the cleric to substitute “the body of a cock and the sole of a shoe,” consigning the odd sacrificial pair to the flames and, for a time at least, laying the boggart. The tale circulated in 19th‑century collections and ballads; John Roby published the Clegg Hall story as part of his Traditions of Lancashire and attributed his version to local writer William Nuttall. ([readingroo.ms](https://readingroo.ms/2/5/2/5/25256/25256-h/25256-h.htm))

The narrative has many variants. In some tellings the murderer throws the children from a gallery into a moat; in William Nuttall’s ballad the killer (named Sir Roland) stabs the children in their beds. The detail about the cock and the shoe sole appears across several 19th‑century folkloric sources and commentators who recorded local oral tradition. These printed versions are the basis for the familiar formulation rather than any surviving medieval chronicle. ([en.wikisource.org](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lancashire_Legends%2C_Traditions%2C_Pageants%2C_Sports%2C_%26c./Part_1/The_Clegg_Hall_Tragedy))

What is verifiable and what is not must be kept apart. The architecture, listing status, grid reference and later history of the house are recorded facts in the national list entry; the boggart story is a well‑attested piece of Lancashire folklore transmitted in ballads and antiquarian collections from the 19th century onwards. No contemporary 14th‑century legal record or chancery roll has been produced to corroborate the dramatic murders the legend describes, and modern local‑history summaries treat the episode as tradition rather than proven history. ([historicengland.org.uk](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1309615))

Why a cock and a shoe? Folklore scholars note that substituting an animal or an object for a human victim belongs to a wider set of European and British “ghost‑laying” motifs: clever clerics, charms and symbolic offerings are common in household hauntings. The shoe sole has its own superstitious currency in rural ritual — shoes, nails and other humble items often appear in foundation deposits and household charms intended to bind or confuse spirits. The Clegg Hall formula fits that pragmatic, symbolic logic. ([iapsop.com](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1893__dyer___ghost_world.pdf))

For Rochdale readers who want practical facts: Clegg Hall is a protected historic building (Grade II*), recorded on the National Heritage List; the listing notes the building’s early‑17th‑century fabric and cites local histories such as Fishwick and the Victoria County History for earlier ownership and site continuity. Public access is not recorded in the listing and the hall is privately held; sources that summarise the legend list access as unestablished. If you want to see the house from the lane, consult the Historic England entry for the official location and mapping. ([historicengland.org.uk](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1309615))

The story’s power is not in its documentary proof but in its longevity. For centuries neighbours and balladeers reworked the kernels — murdered heirs, a haunted house, a shrewd cleric — until the image of a priest burning a cock and a shoe‑sole became the shorthand for how people once negotiated with the uncanny. Read that way, the Clegg Hall boggart remains useful: a piece of local imagination that tells us about old beliefs, about how communities used story to explain misfortune, and about the folk resources people deployed when formal records told them nothing.

If you want to pursue the sources: start with the Historic England listing for the building and room details (it gives the address, listing date and citations) and then read the 19th‑century collections — Roby and the Lancashire legends and ballads — which preserve the versions that later antiquarians and folklorists quoted. Where a claim cannot be verified from medieval records, this piece treats it as tradition rather than established history. ([historicengland.org.uk](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1309615))
