BREAKING INVESTIGATION: £50.3m owed by Rochdale Council companies as £19.5m Council Tax shortfall raises scrutiny

Rochdale's draft accounts show £50.309m in Upperbanks company loan balances, £2.02m interest added and only £132,000 repaid in 2025/26. We examine the figures, the 2022 decisions and the councillors responsible for oversight.

By Rochdale Daily Investigations 12 Oct 2026 · 20:28 UTC

BREAKING INVESTIGATION | ROCHDALE DAILY

Rochdale Borough Council's own draft accounts reveal that three companies behind the Upperbanks development owed it £50.309 million in contractual loans at 31 March 2026. In the preceding financial year, just £132,000 was repaid while £2.020 million in interest accrued, increasing the total balance from £48.262 million.

The figures sit alongside reporting that the borough accumulated a £19.5 million Council Tax collection shortfall over the decade since 2016. These are very different forms of money: Council Tax is revenue for services, while Upperbanks loans finance long-term property assets and are not necessarily due for repayment now. Yet both invite scrutiny of how the authority manages public finances.

Source: Rochdale Borough Council, draft Statement of Accounts 2025/26, note on soft loans, printed page 81: https://www.rochdale.gov.uk/downloads/file/3217/2025-2026-statement-of-accounts-draft-

Source: Roch Valley Radio, 'Council tax shortfall in Rochdale of almost £20m in 10 years', 12 June 2026: https://www.rochvalleyradio.com/news/local-news/council-tax-shortfall-in-rochdale-of-almost-20m-in-10-years/

WHAT IS UPPERBANKS AND HOW MUCH IS OWED?

The £60 million Upperbanks scheme, beside Rochdale Riverside, comprises 242 rental apartments and a 146-room Hampton by Hilton hotel. The development received more than £17 million from the Future High Streets Fund and over £3 million from Greater Manchester's Brownfield Development Fund. The hotel opened in September 2023.

The council's soft-loan table shows £33.662 million owed by Rochdale BTR Ltd on a property loan and £2.045 million in its working-capital borrowing. Rochdale Hotel Holdings Ltd had a £14.270 million property loan and Rochdale Hotel Ltd owed £332,000 in working capital. Together, these total £50.309 million.

The original loan arrangements included payment-holiday periods under which interest accrued and was added to the loans. The council also confirms the lending was provided below market rates in support of its service priorities. That means a growing balance is not, by itself, proof that any company has missed a scheduled payment. The critical missing information is the agreed repayment profile and actual cash returned to the council.

Source: Council draft accounts, printed page 81: https://www.rochdale.gov.uk/downloads/file/3217/2025-2026-statement-of-accounts-draft-

Source: Rochdale Development Agency, Upperbanks project details: https://investinrochdale.co.uk/regeneration/projects/upperbanks

THE £2 MILLION INTEREST QUESTION

The 2025/26 loan schedule lists £2.020 million of accrued interest, £159,000 in new advances and £132,000 in repayments. Net contractual loans increased by £2.047 million. The £2.020 million interest recorded is more than fifteen times that year's total repayments.

However, the same page contains an apparent inconsistency. Its narrative says 'no new advances were made', while the adjoining table shows £159,000 of advances to Rochdale BTR Ltd's working-capital loan. The council should reconcile the entries and specify whether the amount was new cash, a transfer or an accounting classification.

The accounts also put the soft loans at a far lower carrying amount, £22.690 million, because below-market loans are discounted for accounting purposes. The council says the concessionary benefit to its subsidiaries is treated as an additional investment; the value of that investment is stated as £27.3 million. The difference is not a £27 million write-off: it reflects accounting treatment of long-term, below-market lending.

WHAT ABOUT THE RISK OF NON-REPAYMENT?

The council's draft accounts record a £472,000 expected-credit-loss allowance across relevant loans at 31 March 2026, of which £267,000 relates to the hotel companies, £189,000 to Rochdale BTR Ltd and £16,000 to other loans. The allowance increased by a net £37,000 during 2025/26. This is a forward-looking credit-risk provision, not proof that a borrower has defaulted or that £472,000 has been lost.

Source: Council draft accounts, printed page 89, impairment table: https://www.rochdale.gov.uk/downloads/file/3217/2025-2026-statement-of-accounts-draft-

ARE THE APARTMENTS AND HOTEL DELIVERING?

According to Rochdale Development Agency's fourth-quarter delivery plan, 155 of the 242 Upperbanks apartments were occupied on 24 March 2026, with eight more reserved. That left 87 unoccupied at the reporting date, although occupancy had improved from earlier in the year. The agency also reported improving hotel occupancy, average room rates and yields. The public report did not supply a complete profit-and-loss or cash-flow analysis establishing whether the companies were meeting their initial business cases.

Source: Rochdale Development Agency 2025/26 Q4 delivery plan: https://rochdale.moderngov.co.uk/documents/s122466/2025-26%20RDA%20Delivery%20Plan%20Q4%20FINAL.pdf

WHICH COUNCILLORS WERE INVOLVED — AND WHICH PARTIES?

The scheme's corporate and funding decisions were considered under a Labour-controlled Rochdale Council led by Councillor Neil Emmott (Labour). Councillor John Blundell (Labour and Co-operative) held the Economy and Regeneration portfolio in 2022, when the Upperbanks proposals came before Cabinet. Councillor Carol Wardle (Labour) held the Finance and Corporate portfolio then and continues as the Finance and Growth portfolio holder in 2026.

Councillor Daniel Meredith (Labour and Co-operative), now the Cabinet Member for Regeneration and Housing, has a different documented voting history: minutes of the 22 November 2022 Cabinet meeting state that he expressly requested his vote AGAINST the Upperbanks loan agreement be recorded. The minutes do not specify why he objected, and he must not be portrayed as having approved that agreement.

The 2022 decision approved a loan agreement while delegating the detail of its terms and structure to officers in consultation with relevant portfolio holders. The politicians' portfolios identify responsibility for decisions and oversight, but do not individually establish blame for any loss or wrongdoing.

Source: Rochdale Council, Cabinet portfolio responsibilities: https://rochdale.moderngov.co.uk/mgCommitteeDetails.aspx?ID=116

WHY ARE FINANCIAL FORECASTS RESTRICTED?

On 5 February 2026, Cabinet received an Upperbanks trading-company financial update including forecasts compared with the business cases approved in June 2022. The published decision notes that the council is the ultimate parent undertaking and 100% shareholder. But detailed financial enclosures were restricted from public view, leaving the public unable to independently compare the original profitability, occupancy and repayment forecasts with current performance.

Source: Cabinet decision, 5 February 2026: https://rochdale.moderngov.co.uk/ieDecisionDetails.aspx?AIId=49322

THE COUNCIL TAX COMPARISON — WHAT IT DOES AND DOES NOT SHOW

Roch Valley Radio reported that the £19.5 million figure was a cumulative Council Tax collection shortfall since 2016, drawn from 2024/25 accounts, including about £1.3 million in 2024/25. It is not evidence of a fresh £19.5 million hole in the council's 2026/27 budget, and should not be directly subtracted from or compared with Upperbanks' contractual loan book as though both were immediately available cash.

The comparison does raise a narrower, evidence-based question: how transparent is the council about its own long-term financial commitments, loan repayments and commercial outcomes while it seeks to maximise Council Tax collection?

QUESTIONS REQUIRING ANSWERS

Rochdale Daily is seeking clarity on the contractual repayment timetable, how much loan interest has actually been received in cash, the latest independent valuations of Upperbanks' assets, the trading companies' current profitability, the £159,000 'new advances' discrepancy, and whether a non-confidential comparison with the original 2022 business cases will be released.

Councillor Meredith's recorded vote against the original loan agreement also merits an explanation of his reasons at the time, without assuming those reasons.

Upperbanks has delivered homes and a hotel to the town centre, while published occupancy data show improvement. Neither physical regeneration nor a large outstanding loan balance alone establishes that the scheme has succeeded or failed financially. The public accounts do, however, make a compelling case for clear reporting of when public funds are expected to be repaid and whether the original forecasts are being met.

This investigation is based on the council's DRAFT 2025/26 accounts, which remain subject to audit. Neither the loan balances nor the credit-loss allowances establish unlawful conduct, fraud or improper spending.
