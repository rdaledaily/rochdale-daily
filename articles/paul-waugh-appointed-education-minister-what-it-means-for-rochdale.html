Rochdale MP Paul Waugh appointed education minister: what his new role means for the borough

Paul Waugh remains Rochdale's MP while taking on a second role as a junior education minister. We examine his responsibilities, additional salary and what the appointment could mean locally.

By Rochdale Daily Newsdesk

Rochdale MP Paul Waugh has joined the Government as a Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State in the Department for Education while continuing to represent the constituency in the House of Commons.

The appointment was formally confirmed as part of Prime Minister Andy Burnham's ministerial changes, with Mr Waugh taking up the post on 22 July 2026. Lucy Powell is the Secretary of State for Education and heads the department in which he will serve.

Mr Waugh now holds two connected public roles. He remains Rochdale's elected Member of Parliament, responsible for representing constituents, handling casework, holding surgeries, speaking on local issues and voting in the Commons. Alongside that work, he is now a junior government minister with responsibilities inside the Department for Education.

There is nothing unusual or constitutionally improper about an MP also serving as a minister. Most ministers in the House of Commons continue to represent their constituencies. His appointment does not create a vacancy, require a by-election or transfer his constituency responsibilities to another MP.

However, the ministerial job will substantially increase his workload. Ministers are expected to attend departmental meetings, study submissions from civil servants, help develop policy, answer parliamentary questions, take legislation through Parliament, meet education organisations and defend government decisions publicly.

That means constituents will reasonably expect reassurance that Rochdale casework and local representation will continue at the same level. Much of an MP's casework is managed by constituency and parliamentary staff, but Mr Waugh remains personally accountable to Rochdale voters and will still be expected to maintain a visible presence in the borough.

His precise policy portfolio has not yet been published by the Department for Education. The official government page currently confirms his rank and appointment but does not list a detailed set of responsibilities. It would therefore be premature to claim that he has direct control of a particular area such as schools, special educational needs, colleges or apprenticeships until the department formally allocates those duties.

As a Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State, he is one of the department's junior ministers. Such ministers commonly oversee a defined group of policies, represent the department during Commons debates, answer written and oral questions, meet professional bodies and work under the direction of the Education Secretary and more senior ministers.

The Department for Education is responsible for education and children's services in England. Its work includes early years provision, schools, teacher recruitment and retention, the curriculum, children's social care, further education, higher education, apprenticeships and skills.

The appointment could give Rochdale a stronger connection to national education decision-making, although a minister is required to make decisions for the country as a whole rather than favouring their own constituency.

Locally, there are several areas where residents and education providers will watch his work closely. These include special educational needs and disabilities, school funding, teacher recruitment, attendance, college provision, apprenticeships and support for children requiring speech, language and communication services.

Mr Waugh has previously highlighted Rochdale's work on SEND and early speech and language support. In June, after hosting a ministerial visit to the borough, he said his job was to ensure Rochdale received every penny and every bit of support it had earned. His new position places him inside the department responsible for much of that national policy, although ministerial rules may limit how directly he can intervene in individual local funding decisions.

His salary is also expected to increase if he claims the ministerial salary attached to the post. All MPs currently receive a basic annual salary of £98,599. The claimed additional salary for a Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State serving in the Commons is £22,375.

That would produce total gross annual pay of £120,974 before tax, National Insurance and pension deductions. Ministerial salaries are separate from parliamentary expenses, which are intended to cover legitimate costs such as employing staff, running offices and travelling for parliamentary duties rather than providing additional personal income.

The increase is paid because ministerial office carries responsibilities beyond those of an ordinary backbench MP. Ministers are members of the Government and are bound by collective responsibility, meaning they are generally expected to support agreed government policy publicly or resign from office.

That obligation can occasionally create tension between the two roles. As Rochdale's MP, Mr Waugh is expected to raise local concerns and challenge decisions affecting his constituents. As a minister, he must also support the Government's education programme and comply with the Ministerial Code.

Having a minister as the local MP can provide greater access to senior figures and a deeper understanding of how government decisions are made. It does not, however, guarantee preferential funding or special treatment for the constituency. Decisions involving grants, schools and public bodies should continue to follow proper departmental processes.

Mr Waugh said he was "delighted" to be joining the Department for Education. His own website announced the appointment on 23 July, a day after the official government record says he took up the role.

The official announcement confirms that he joined an education team led by Lucy Powell, who became Education Secretary on 20 July. Other ministers in the department include Georgia Gould, Baroness Smith of Malvern and Josh MacAlister.

For Rochdale, the main test will be whether Mr Waugh can manage the demands of government while continuing to respond effectively to residents. Constituents should still contact his office about immigration cases, benefits, housing, public services and other matters requiring assistance from their MP.

His appointment raises Rochdale's profile within Westminster and places its MP closer to decisions affecting schools, children and skills across England. The title alone will not determine whether the borough benefits. That will depend on the responsibilities he is eventually given, the policies he helps deliver and whether residents continue to receive effective representation from their MP.
