Does a boggart still haunt Clegg Hall?

The house on Clegg Hall Road is real and protected, the boggart story is old — but the medieval “tragedy” that feeds the legend is not supported by contemporary records.

By Rochdale Daily 27 Oct 2026 · 16:35 UTC

Short answer: there is no documentary proof that Clegg Hall was the scene of a medieval massacre and therefore no factual basis to claim a still‑active, historically verified haunting. The house that people call Clegg Hall survives as a recognisable historic building and the boggart tale survives as folklore recorded by Victorian collectors; the two things sit side by side, not one inside the other. ([historicengland.org.uk](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1309615))

What we can say with confidence about place and period is plainly written in the records. The standing house on Clegg Hall Road is Grade II* on the National Heritage List for England and is described there as a house of about 1610, probably built for Theophilus Ashton; the list entry records its form, materials and a National Grid Reference (SD 92242 14490). The List entry is concerned with architecture and history, not ghost stories. ([historicengland.org.uk](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1309615))

The boggart story — the thing people mean when they talk about “Clegg Hall” in the way they might say “that place is haunted” — is a well‑attested piece of Lancashire folklore. It was written down and popularised in the nineteenth century by folklorists and local balladists. John Roby included an extended narrative about the “Clegg‑Hall Boggart” in his Traditions of Lancashire, and nineteenth‑century collectors and compilers (and later commentators) preserved variants that add striking motifs: murdered heirs, demons walking the house, an exorcist who bargains for “a body and a soul” and then produces “the body of a cock and the sole of a shoe” as a trick to lay the spirits. Those details recur across Roby, local ballads and later folklore anthologies. ([gutenberg.org](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/25256/25256-h/25256-h.htm))

That still leaves a gap between colourful tale and verifiable fact. County historians who examined surviving deeds, visitations and surveys note that the estate’s documentary trail is ordinary land‑transaction history: the Clegg estates passed through Belfield, Howorth and other families, and the present house is likely a seventeenth‑century rebuild rather than a thirteenth‑ or fourteenth‑century timber manor in which a serial murder took place. The 1889 History of the Parish of Rochdale specifically warns that Roby’s dramatic narrative is “pure imagination” while acknowledging a living tradition of a ghost and even a local proverb, “As ill as Clegg Hall boggart,” meaning hard to get rid of. In short, land deeds and the Manor Survey (1626) document ownership and a substantial house; they do not record the alleged atrocity that underpins the legend. ([your-content.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com](https://your-content.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/04145039/chapter-xvii-old-houses-and-old-families-butterworth-pages337-389.pdf))

How historians and folklorists read that difference matters. Folklore scholars treat the Clegg Hall boggart as a regional example of a wider boggart tradition in Lancashire — mischievous, sometimes malevolent household spirits attached to particular places — and they trace how oral tale, ballad and antiquarian retelling built up the colourful episodes now associated with the site. Local‑history sources, by contrast, look for contemporary legal or parish records to corroborate dramatic claims and find none that support a medieval massacre. Both approaches are valid but answer different questions: one asks how people told and remembered the place, the other asks what can be proved from surviving documents. ([gutenberg.org](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/25256/25256-h/25256-h.htm))

So does a boggart still haunt Clegg Hall? If you mean “is there corroborated historical evidence of a medieval tragedy that left a ghost,” the answer is no. If you mean “does the story still haunt local memory and appear in collections and ballads?” the answer is yes — the tale is part of Rochdale‑area folklore and was repeatedly collected and retold in the nineteenth century and later. The difference is between living local tradition and verifiable historical fact. ([gutenberg.org](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/25256/25256-h/25256-h.htm))

If you want to pursue the matter further: the National Heritage List entry is the authoritative source for the building’s architecture and dating; nineteenth‑century folkloric accounts (Roby and others) are the primary sources for the boggart narrative; and county histories and deed records (summarised in the parish history) are the place to check whether documentary evidence exists for any supposed crime. The List entry and county history are the best starting points for anyone who prefers deeds to apparitions. ([historicengland.org.uk](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1309615))
