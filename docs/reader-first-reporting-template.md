# Rochdale Daily: the reader-first reporting template

Applies to new articles and material updates. Do not rewrite the historical archive merely for style or keywords. This guide complements docs/editorial-voice-rochdale-daily.md and the accuracy, privacy and legal rules in editorial standards.

## The newsroom question

After reading our report, can someone who lives, works, studies or travels in Rochdale borough make sense of what has happened and know what to do next, if action is needed?

**Never create a fact to fill a section.** Do not force all sections onto every piece. A 90-word verified report can be better than a 700-word story with invented explanations.

## Flexible article structure

1. **Headline:** the exact development and a searchable real place or organisation when known. Avoid hollow labels, capitals, clickbait and keyword strings.
2. **Standfirst:** a fresh useful fact beyond the headline. Specify a date, named location, scope or practical implication if supported.
3. **First paragraph:** the news, in natural British English. What changed? Where and when? Who confirmed it?
4. **Reader impact:** who may be affected? The road, bus service, school, business, household, neighbourhood or deadline. No speculative effects dressed as fact.
5. **Evidence:** linked records, attributed statements, precise figures with units and dates. Explain what the evidence does and does not establish.
6. **Context:** relevant background, honest comparisons and past decisions where documented. Avoid stock filler and sweeping generalisations.
7. **What happens next:** a scheduled event, confirmed next step, response deadline, relevant official service or how to get help. If there is no verified next step, end with the facts rather than making one up.
8. **Navigation:** primary source link, correction route and relevant local coverage where actually useful. Keep the same permanent article URL for substantial updates.

These are reporting questions, **not eight compulsory subheadings**. Short, urgent briefs normally need a clear headline, a verified lead and one paragraph of practical facts. Longer investigations may use two or three descriptive section headings to help readers scan evidence.

## Tailor by subject

| Coverage | The reader should be able to find |
|---|---|
| Traffic or transport | Exact road or service number, affected section, direction, effective time, expected end, official diversion or operator updates, if verified |
| Council and public finance | Decision or allegation status, period, comparable numbers, primary documents, response from responsible organisation, planned next decision |
| Health and education | Which facility or children are affected, dates, eligibility and public service contact or appeal route from official sources |
| Crime and courts | Police or court attribution, exact procedural stage, lawful appeal details when an appeal exists, no prejudicial speculation |
| Local businesses | Actual address, products or services, opening times, prices, jobs and how to visit or contact, only where sourced |
| What's on and family events | Place, date, start time, cost, booking requirements, suitable age range and accessibility when confirmed |
| Environment | Named site, evidence period, verified measurements, recognised official guidance and next confirmed assessment |
| Sport | Result, competition, relevant standings and next fixture with dates if confirmed |
| Community support | Who qualifies, how to attend or volunteer, opening times, cost and a verified contact route |

## Worked example: a road closure

Do not write: "Motorists are being advised to plan ahead as significant disruption is expected on a major road in Rochdale. The changes highlight the importance of road safety."

Instead, once the council order is obtained, report:

* Which named road and precise stretch the order covers.
* Exactly when the restriction starts and is expected to end.
* Whether access for residents, buses and emergency services is addressed in the order.
* The official signed diversion, if published.
* The reason recorded by the authority, not a guessed motive.
* A link to the original notice or transport update.

If the official notice does not identify a diversion, say that it **does not specify one**, rather than inventing directions. Never describe a road as closed now merely because a future order exists.

## Search usefulness

The title must match a real reader question but not slavishly repeat it. Use names, streets, wards, dates and other relevant terms naturally. Create meaningful section and ward hubs that link to reports people can actually read. A current article, a corrected article and its original publication date should remain tied to one canonical URL.

Sitemaps help discovery; they do not certify indexing, traffic or rankings. Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools must be used to measure actual query impressions, position, click-through rate, indexing and missed searches. Original reporting, completeness and trust are more valuable than repetitive keyword placement.

## Reporter sign-off

Before publication ask: Does the lead answer the headline? Does the second paragraph add anything? Is the time frame clear? Could the reader act safely on the practical details? Have quotes and figures been checked? Is the source identifiable? Is it clear what remains unknown? Could any paragraph have been pasted into a totally unrelated story? Have we unnecessarily exposed a vulnerable person?

If a practical claim cannot be verified, leave it out and seek the source, rather than padding the article. The goal is the **most useful *verified* account**, not the longest article.
