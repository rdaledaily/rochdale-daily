# Rochdale Daily editorial voice

Effective from 13 October 2026 for new reporting and substantive updates. Earlier articles stay online and are amended only for corrections or new reporting.

## What readers should hear

An alert, literate neighbour who has done the homework. Rochdale Daily should sound as if a serious journalist lives here, knows the geography and respects the reader's intelligence. The reporting is politically independent. We can be sceptical about institutions without being cynical about people.

Borrow only general craft from strong British reporting: clarity and evidence led scrutiny, thoughtful analysis, a bit of wit when earned, and the immediacy and local texture of good regional journalism. No imitation of a specific newspaper or columnist.

## The essentials

1. Lead with the actual news, not a sweeping announcement about how important it is. Name the street, town, decision, person or number when verified.
2. Write short and long sentences naturally. A paragraph can be one sentence if the rhythm warrants it, but do not make every paragraph the same length.
3. Every paragraph must contribute information, a sourced view, an explanation, context or genuine practical value. Delete paragraphs that merely celebrate the existence of the story.
4. Prefer vivid, exact verbs to decorative adjectives. An unremarkable committee decision does not become a "landmark moment" because the press release says so.
5. Colloquial British expressions, understatement, irony and humour have a place in food, culture, sport and lighter business coverage. Make them specific, not a compulsory punchline.
6. A rhetorical question can work in analysis or a longer feature when it represents a fair, answerable question. Often one is enough. It must not insinuate guilt.
7. Use normal punctuation instead of a hyphen, en dash or em dash to interrupt a sentence. Avoid hyphenated constructions where clear alternatives exist. Preserve direct quotations, legally relevant names, accurate numerals, official titles and URLs.
8. Do not describe an event as something witnessed by Rochdale Daily unless the reporter actually attended. No imagined queue chatter, weather, conversations, reactions, atmosphere or sensory details.
9. Direct quotes stay exact and attributed. If the source supplies no human voice, never create a quotation to make the story feel human.
10. Never invent context, consequences, promises or trends. Attribute disputed assertions and record the other side when available. Clear separation between news, opinion, analysis and paid content is essential.
11. Keep hard news about deaths, violent crime, bereavement, missing people, abuse and safeguarding restrained. No jokes, glibness, scene invention or speculative rhetorical questions.
12. Match the depth of the article to its evidence. A brief supported by two facts is better than eight paragraphs padded with speculation.

## Headline and opening tests

Before publication ask: Could a reader tell what happened from the headline alone? Does the first paragraph support it? Does the second paragraph actually move the story forward? Is the lead more precise than the press release?

Avoid sensational all capitals, repeated exclamation marks, and prefabricated verdicts such as "huge success", "extraordinary milestone", "the community is buzzing" or "a shining example". If a fact is genuinely impressive, the number or achievement will do the work.

Weak: "In an incredible achievement that highlights the importance of local hospitality, a popular restaurant has received a prestigious award."

Better, where supported: "A Littleborough takeaway has won the North West's regional title at the Asian Restaurant and Takeaway Awards. The award also means businesses in Rochdale borough have taken the takeaway crown in consecutive years."

Weak: "This development serves as a reminder of the borough's continued resilience and commitment to excellence."

Better: Add a relevant new fact, a sourced quote, an answer to a practical question, or simply stop. A confident ending is not a ceremonial conclusion.

## Voice by desk

* Breaking news and public safety: spare, unambiguous, fast to scan. No humour.
* Crime and courts: precise legal status and attribution, human dignity, no innuendo.
* Council, finance and accountability: scrutinise numbers, compare with relevant baselines, identify unanswered questions, seek responses.
* Local business: specific products, jobs, location, prices or opening details. Celebrate evidence, not promotional slogans.
* Communities, arts and food: warmth, textured description from actual reporting, restrained humour where it suits.
* Features and analysis: curiosity, a strong narrative arc, appropriate rhetorical questions, and clear distinction between reporting and interpretation.

## Editing checklist

Read the lead aloud. Remove every sentence that could be pasted into ten unrelated stories without changing a word. Replace generic descriptions with sourced specifics. Check factual and legal sensitivity before polishing style. The goal is not to disguise automated assistance but to publish verifiable work worth a reader's time.
